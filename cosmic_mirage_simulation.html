What Am I Looking At?

The main window is a telescope's view into deep space. It's filled with distant galaxies. Your job is to create a cosmic string and see how it affects the light from those galaxies. A cosmic string is a hypothetical, incredibly thin crack in spacetime left over from the early universe. It's completely invisible, but its gravity bends light in a very peculiar way.

Analogy: The Funhouse Mirror of Spacetime

Imagine gravity as a funhouse mirror. A massive object like a star or a black hole creates a complex, distorted reflection. It might stretch, magnify, or curve the image of things behind it.

A cosmic string is different. It's not like a curved mirror. It's more like taking a perfectly flat mirror, cutting it in half, and then joining the two pieces back together at a slight angle. The reflection you see isn't distorted or magnified—it's just duplicated. This simulation lets you play with that unique "doubling" effect.

The Physics of Conical Spacetime

Part A: Spacetime for Laypeople

Einstein's General Relativity tells us that space and time are woven together into a single fabric: spacetime. Massive objects warp this fabric. The classic analogy is a bowling ball on a rubber sheet. The ball creates a dip, and smaller marbles rolling nearby will have their paths curved by this dip. This curvature is what we experience as gravity.

A star, a planet, or a black hole creates a positive curvature, like a dimple in the sheet. Light, just like the marbles, follows these curves. This is why a black hole can bend light so dramatically, creating beautiful arcs and rings (called Einstein Rings) from the light of galaxies behind it.

Part B: The Deficit Angle - A Slice of Pi

A cosmic string is fundamentally different. It's an object with immense tension but very little mass per unit length. It doesn't create a dimple in spacetime. Instead, it creates a "topological defect."

Imagine spacetime as a perfectly flat sheet of paper. Now, take a pair of scissors, cut out a very thin wedge (like a tiny slice of pizza), and tape the two cut edges back together. What do you have? A cone. This is conical spacetime. The key features are:

  • It's Flat: Everywhere on the cone's surface (except the very tip), the geometry is flat. A 2D creature living on it could draw triangles whose angles add up to 180 degrees. This is why the lensed images from a cosmic string are not distorted or magnified.
  • It's Missing an Angle: The total angle around the cone's center is less than 360 degrees. The amount of the angle that's "missing" is called the deficit angle (δφ).

This deficit angle is directly proportional to the string's tension, a value represented by the dimensionless quantity Gμ (where G is Newton's constant and μ is the mass per unit length). The formula is simple: δφ = 8πGμ. When light from a distant galaxy passes on either side of the string, it travels on this conical surface. From our perspective, the two paths that reach our telescope appear to come from two different locations, creating a double image. The angular separation between these two images is determined by the deficit angle and the distances involved.

Part C: Lensing Compared

Image Properties Magnified, stretched, and distorted (shear). No magnification, no distortion. Perfect copies.
Chromatic Effect Achromatic (all colors bent equally). Achromatic for a stationary string. Red/blueshift for a moving string.

Part D: The Kaiser-Stebbins Effect

What happens when the string moves across our line of sight? This is where things get even more interesting. As the string moves, it drags its conical spacetime with it. Consider the two paths light can take from a galaxy to you.

  • Light passing on the side of the string that is moving towards the light path gets a gravitational "kick" or blueshift. It gains energy. This image appears slightly bluer and brighter.
  • Light passing on the side that is moving away from the light path gets a gravitational "drag" or redshift. It loses energy. This image appears slightly redder and fainter.

This phenomenon is known as the Kaiser-Stebbins effect. It is a powerful, unique signature. Finding a pair of identical, unmagnified galaxy images where one is brighter and bluer than the other would be smoking-gun evidence for a moving cosmic string. The brightness ratio is directly related to the string's velocity component perpendicular to the line of sight (v⊥), and the Lorentz factor γ = 1/sqrt(1-v²). The frequency (and thus brightness) is shifted by a factor of 1 ± 4πGμv⊥γ.

Advanced Extensions & Maximal Depth

The simulation perfectly models an idealized, straight cosmic string. But what if the strings are more complex? The universe is a messy place, after all. Here we explore the "maximal depth" questions.

Kinks: Whips of Spacetime

Cosmic strings are not necessarily perfectly straight. They can have sharp bends called kinks. A kink is not just a static feature; it's a wave that travels along the string at the speed of light. Imagine cracking a whip—the sharp bend flies down its length.

How would a kink affect lensing? As a kink passes through the line of sight to a background galaxy, it would create a rapid, localized change in the lensing signature. The geometry of the cone would be momentarily and violently perturbed in that specific direction. An observer might see the two lensed images of a galaxy suddenly shift, brighten, or even briefly merge and split again. The timescale of this change would be extremely short, dictated by the light-speed passage of the kink. Detecting such a flicker would be an incredible observational challenge, but it would provide a wealth of information about string dynamics.

Loops: Gravitational Jewels

What if a string bites its own tail, forming a closed loop? These cosmic string loops are fascinating objects. They oscillate, vibrate, and decay by radiating gravitational waves. Their lensing signature is far more complex and beautiful than a straight string's.

A source located outside the loop's projection on the sky would see the standard two images, as if it were just a segment of a long string. However, a background galaxy located inside the projected area of the loop would have its light lensed in multiple ways. The exact number of images depends on the loop's complex shape and the source's position. A simple, flat, circular loop could produce four images of a source near its center. As the loop oscillates, vibrates, and rotates, these images would dance around, changing in brightness, appearing, and disappearing. A sufficiently advanced observatory could potentially reconstruct the 3D shape and motion of the loop just by tracking the dance of these lensed images—a true feat of gravitational cartography.

Microlensing: A String's Stellar Eclipse

Galaxies are large and fuzzy. What if a string passes in front of a single, point-like star? This is called microlensing. Unlike lensing by a compact object (like a rogue planet or a MACHO), which causes a characteristic brightening and fading as it passes, a string would produce a different light curve.

As the string approaches the star's line of sight, nothing happens. Then, suddenly, a second image of the star appears. The total brightness of the star abruptly doubles (since there are now two stars of the same brightness). This brightness remains constant for the entire duration of the string's passage. Then, just as suddenly, as the string moves off the line of sight, the second image vanishes, and the total brightness drops back to normal. The resulting light curve is a perfect "top-hat" or rectangular shape. This is a unique and unambiguous signal. Standard microlensing events have a smooth, bell-shaped curve (a "Paczyński curve"). Seeing a top-hat light curve would be another smoking-gun piece of evidence for a cosmic string.

In-Depth Q&A

1. Why is there absolutely no magnification or distortion? This seems counter-intuitive for gravitational lensing.

This is the most profound difference between string lensing and conventional lensing. The key lies in the nature of the spacetime curvature. A massive object like a star or black hole creates what's called 'positive' curvature. It actively focuses light rays, like a glass lens. This focusing action is what causes magnification (making the source appear larger) and shear (stretching it into arcs). The more the light is bent, the more it is focused, and the greater the magnification.

A cosmic string, however, does not curve spacetime in this way. Spacetime *around* the string is perfectly flat. The string's effect is purely topological. The "deficit angle" means that spacetime is a cone. Light rays travel in perfect straight lines across this flat surface. They are not 'focused'. Imagine two straight lines drawn from a point on a flat piece of paper. They diverge. Now, cut a wedge out of that paper and form a cone. The two lines are still locally straight, but now they can both be aimed at a single observer from different directions. The observer sees two images, but because the light rays were never focused, the images have the exact same size and shape as the original source. It's a purely geometric effect of having two distinct "straight-line" paths in a non-trivial topology.

2. What are cosmic strings made of? If they have tension, what holds them together?

Cosmic strings are not made of matter in the conventional sense (like atoms or particles). They are believed to be "topological defects" in the quantum fields that permeate the universe. This requires a bit of an analogy. Imagine water freezing into ice. As crystals form and grow, they might meet at boundaries where the crystal lattices don't perfectly align. These boundaries are defects—2D "domain walls" in this case.

In the very early universe, as it cooled after the Big Bang, it's thought to have undergone a series of "phase transitions," similar to water freezing. These transitions involved the separation of fundamental forces and the settling of quantum fields (like the Higgs field) into their low-energy states. A cosmic string is a 1D line-like defect left over from such a transition. It's a region where the quantum field is "stuck" in a high-energy, symmetric state from the early universe, while everything around it has settled. This trapped line of high-energy vacuum is what we call a cosmic string. Its tension comes from this trapped energy, and it's "held together" by the lower-energy vacuum state of the rest of the universe surrounding it.

3. The slider for Gμ goes up to 10⁻⁶. Is this a realistic value? What are the observational limits?

The value Gμ = 10⁻⁶ is actually the upper limit of what is considered plausible, and it serves as a great tool for making the lensing effect dramatic and easy to see in this simulation. For a long time, this was the value predicted by many Grand Unified Theories (GUTs).

However, real-world observations have placed very strong constraints on this value. The primary constraint comes from the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB), the afterglow of the Big Bang. If cosmic strings with such high tension existed, they would leave detectable imprints on the CMB's temperature fluctuations. The Planck satellite, which mapped the CMB with incredible precision, found no evidence of these signatures, ruling out Gμ values around 10⁻⁶ or 10⁻⁷. Further constraints come from pulsar timing arrays. The gravitational waves emitted by oscillating string loops would create a stochastic background that would affect the timing of pulsar signals. The absence of this signal further constrains Gμ. Current limits place Gμ at less than about 10⁻¹⁰ or even 10⁻¹¹. So, in reality, the lensing effect would be incredibly subtle, with image separations far smaller than an arcsecond, making it extremely difficult to detect.

4. Could we ever use a cosmic string for something, like space travel?

This is a fascinating science fiction concept. The conical spacetime around a string has a tantalizing property. If you travel around the string, the total angle is less than 360 degrees. This means that you could travel in a circle around the string and arrive back at your starting point having traveled a shorter distance than you would have in normal flat space. In principle, two spaceships could meet by traveling around opposite sides of a string and arrive in less time than a direct flight.

Furthermore, some theories involving "closed timelike curves" (a pathway to travel into the past) use the geometry of two moving cosmic strings passing each other at relativistic speeds. However, these scenarios are purely theoretical and face immense problems. First, we've never found a cosmic string. Second, the energy required to manipulate them would be astronomical. Third, quantum effects and stability issues would almost certainly prevent such exotic travel. So, for now, it remains a beautiful piece of theoretical physics and a great plot device, but not a practical engineering goal.

5. How is the deficit angle δφ = 8πGμ derived? Let's see a calculation.

The derivation is a classic result from General Relativity. It involves solving Einstein's field equations for the specific case of a long, straight string. The energy-momentum tensor for an idealized string along the z-axis has only two non-zero components: tension in the z-direction (Tzz) and energy density (Ttt). For a relativistic string, these are equal in magnitude: Ttt = -Tzz = μ.

One then solves for the spacetime metric. The resulting metric, in cylindrical coordinates (r, φ, z), looks surprisingly simple: ds² = dt² - dz² - dr² - (1 - 4Gμ)²r²dφ². This metric describes flat spacetime everywhere. However, notice the term `(1 - 4Gμ)²r²dφ²`. In normal flat space, this would just be `r²dφ²`. The term `(1 - 4Gμ)` acts as a scaling factor on the azimuthal coordinate φ. If we define a new angular coordinate `φ' = (1 - 4Gμ)φ`, the metric becomes standard flat space. But what is the range of this new coordinate? As the original φ goes from 0 to 2π, the new φ' only goes from 0 to 2π(1 - 4Gμ). The total angle is no longer 2π. The deficit angle, δφ, is the amount "missing":
δφ = 2π - 2π(1 - 4Gμ) = 2π - 2π + 8πGμ = 8πGμ.

Let's calculate it for a GUT-scale string with Gμ = 10⁻⁶:
δφ = 8 * π * 10⁻⁶ radians ≈ 2.51 x 10⁻⁵ radians.
To convert to arcseconds: (2.51 x 10⁻⁵ rad) * (180/π degrees/rad) * (3600 arcsec/degree) ≈ 5.2 arcseconds. This is a very detectable separation, which is why this value has been observationally ruled out.

6. Why do the lensed images appear on opposite sides of the string?

This is a direct consequence of the conical geometry. Imagine you are the observer, and a galaxy is the source, with the string's vertex positioned between you. In normal space, there is only one straight line connecting you and the galaxy. On a cone, there are two. Think of "unrolling" the cone back into a flat sheet with a missing wedge. The observer and the source are now points on this sheet. The original line of sight is blocked by the "vertex" (where the string is). However, a new straight-line path now exists across the area where the wedge was removed. In fact, two such paths exist, one going around each of the identified edges of the wedge. When you "re-roll" the paper into a cone, these two straight lines become the two light paths that travel on opposite sides of the string. From the observer's point of view, looking back along these two paths, the light appears to originate from two different locations in the sky, symmetric with respect to the string's actual position.

7. What would happen if two cosmic strings crossed?

When two strings cross, they "intercommute" or "reconnect." Imagine two strings approaching each other. At the point of intersection, they exchange partners. If you have string AB and string CD crossing, they will reconnect to form strings AD and CB. This process is fundamental to the evolution of a cosmic string network. It allows long strings to chop each other up and create closed loops. These loops can then oscillate, decay via gravitational radiation, and eventually disappear. This is a key mechanism that prevents a network of cosmic strings from completely dominating the energy density of the universe. The kinks mentioned in the Advanced Extensions tab are often formed at the point of reconnection, propagating away from the intersection at the speed of light.

8. How does the brightness ratio in the Kaiser-Stebbins effect depend on velocity? Give me the numbers.

The effect is a relativistic Doppler shift. The frequency of light from the two images is shifted up or down. The ratio of the observed frequency (ν) to the source frequency (ν₀) for each image is approximately ν/ν₀ = 1 ± 4πGμv⊥γ where v⊥ is the string's velocity perpendicular to the line of sight (in units of c), and γ is the Lorentz factor 1/√(1-v²). Since brightness is related to the energy of the photons, this frequency shift translates directly to a brightness change.

Let's take Gμ = 10⁻⁸ and a velocity of 50% the speed of light (v = 0.5c). The Lorentz factor γ is 1/√(1-0.5²) ≈ 1.15.
The frequency shift factor is: 4 * π * 10⁻⁸ * 0.5 * 1.15 ≈ 7.23 x 10⁻⁸.
The brightness of the approaching image is proportional to (1 + 7.23e-8), and the receding image is (1 - 7.23e-8).
The ratio of their brightnesses is (1 + 7.23e-8) / (1 - 7.23e-8) ≈ 1.00000014. This is an incredibly small effect!
This is why the simulation uses higher Gμ values to make the effect visible. Let's re-run with Gμ = 10⁻⁶ and v = 0.5c.
The shift factor is: 4 * π * 10⁻⁶ * 0.5 * 1.15 ≈ 7.23 x 10⁻⁶.
The brightness ratio is (1 + 7.23e-6) / (1 - 7.23e-6) ≈ 1.000014. Still tiny! The simulation exaggerates this effect for educational purposes, but in reality, it would be a miniscule photometric difference.

9. Could a cosmic string be dark matter?

No, at least not the majority of it. Dark matter is known to clump gravitationally, forming the massive, diffuse halos around galaxies and galaxy clusters. Its gravitational effects are seen on vast scales. Cosmic strings, if they exist, would form a sparse network throughout the universe. While they have mass and exert gravity, their contribution to the total mass density of the universe is thought to be very small, far less than the ~27% required for dark matter. Their gravitational effects are also very different—the sharp, linear lensing signature is unlike the smooth, large-scale lensing caused by dark matter halos. So, while they are a "dark" form of mass-energy, they are a candidate for a tiny fraction of the universe's energy budget, not the solution to the entire dark matter problem.

10. If a string passes in front of the Cosmic Microwave Background, what does it do?

This is one of the primary methods used to search for cosmic strings. A string would create a linear discontinuity in the temperature of the CMB. As the string moves across the sky, the Kaiser-Stebbins effect comes into play. On one side of the string, the CMB photons are blueshifted (made hotter), and on the other side, they are redshifted (made colder). This isn't a gradual change; it's a sharp, step-like edge in temperature along the line of the string. An observer like the Planck satellite would see a long, thin line across the CMB map with a temperature on one side being slightly but uniformly higher than the temperature on the other. The magnitude of this temperature jump is given by ΔT/T = 8πGμv⊥γ. Searches for these specific linear discontinuities in the high-precision CMB maps have been one of the most powerful tools for constraining the existence of high-tension cosmic strings, and their absence has led to the very low upper limits on Gμ we have today.
