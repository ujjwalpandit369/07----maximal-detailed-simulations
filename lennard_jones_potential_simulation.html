Beginner’s Guide to Interatomic Forces and Potentials

Welcome to the fascinating world of the unseen! At every moment, countless atoms and molecules are interacting all around you and within you. The air you breathe, the water you drink, the chair you're sitting on—they all hold their shape and have their properties because of the tiny forces between their constituent particles. This simulation explores one of the most fundamental models used to describe these interactions: the Lennard-Jones potential.

But what are these forces, and what is a "potential"? Let's start from scratch.

What is a Force?

In everyday life, a force is simply a push or a pull. You push a door open, gravity pulls a ball to the ground, a magnet pulls on a piece of iron. At the atomic scale, it's the same idea. Two atoms can pull each other closer together (an attractive force) or push each other apart (a repulsive force). These forces are electromagnetic in nature, arising from the interactions between the positively charged nuclei and the negatively charged electrons of the atoms.

What is a Potential? (Potential Energy)

Now, imagine lifting that ball off the ground. You have to do work against the force of gravity. In doing so, you give the ball potential energy. It has the "potential" to fall back down and release that energy as motion. The higher you lift it, the more potential energy it has. A potential energy "landscape" is a way to describe the forces at every point in space. A steep slope on this landscape means a strong force, while a flat area means a weak or zero force. Objects naturally "want" to move to a position of lower potential energy, just as a ball rolls downhill.

The Lennard-Jones potential is exactly this: a map that tells us the potential energy between two neutral atoms at any given distance from each other. By looking at the "shape" of this potential energy map (the graph in the simulation), we can understand the forces between the atoms.

  • If the graph is sloping downwards, the force is attractive (pulling the atoms together, like a ball rolling downhill).
  • If the graph is sloping upwards, the force is repulsive (pushing them apart, like trying to push a ball up a steep hill).
  • If the graph is flat, there is no force. The bottom of the "valley" in the graph is the most stable position, where the attractive and repulsive forces are perfectly balanced.

The Two Big Ideas: Attraction and Repulsion

The Lennard-Jones model is built on a beautiful and intuitive balance between two opposing forces:

  1. Long-Range Attraction: When two atoms are moderately far apart, they gently pull on each other. This is a bit like weak, "sticky" gravity. This attraction is due to tiny, fleeting fluctuations in the electron clouds of the atoms, creating temporary dipoles that induce similar dipoles in their neighbors. This is known as the London dispersion force, a type of van der Waals force. It's why gases can condense into liquids if you cool them down enough—the atoms start to stick together.
  2. Short-Range Repulsion: When you try to push two atoms *very* close together, they strongly resist. It's like trying to squash two solid billiard balls into each other. This powerful repulsive force comes from a fundamental rule of quantum mechanics called the Pauli Exclusion Principle, which states that the electron clouds of the atoms cannot occupy the same space. This force is what prevents you from falling through the floor; the atoms in your shoes are being forcefully repelled by the atoms in the ground.

The simulation above allows you to play with this balance. As you change the distance `r`, you are moving the particles along the potential energy curve. Watch how the energy changes. The "sweet spot" is the bottom of the valley, where the particles are most stable and comfortable. This is the equilibrium bond distance in a simple molecule or the average spacing of atoms in a liquid or solid.

Core Theory of the Lennard-Jones Potential

The Lennard-Jones potential is a mathematically simple model that approximates the interaction between a pair of neutral atoms or molecules. It has been incredibly successful in computational chemistry and physics for simulating gases, liquids, and solids, providing valuable insights into their properties from the atomic level up.

The Lennard-Jones Equation

The potential energy `V` as a function of the distance `r` between the centers of the two particles is given by the formula:

V(r) = 4ε [ (σ/r)12 - (σ/r)6 ]

Let's break down every single piece of this equation in extreme detail:

  • V(r): This is the output of the function, the potential energy at a given separation distance `r`. A negative value implies a net attractive interaction (a stable bound state), while a positive value implies a net repulsive interaction. A value of zero means the attractive and repulsive components are balanced in a specific way, but it does not mean there is no force.
  • r: This is the main input to the function, the distance between the centers of the two interacting particles. In the simulation, you control this directly with the 'Distance (r)' slider.
  • ε (Epsilon): This parameter is the depth of the potential well. It represents the maximum energy of attraction between the particles. A larger `ε` means a stronger bond or "stickiness" between the particles. You can think of it as the amount of energy required to pull the two particles apart from their most stable configuration (at the bottom of the well) to an infinite distance. In the simulation, you control this with the 'Epsilon (ε)' slider. Its units are energy, typically Joules or electronvolts.
  • σ (Sigma): This parameter is the finite distance at which the potential energy is zero. It is not the point of zero force, nor is it the collision diameter in a hard-sphere sense. It gives a measure of the effective "size" of the atom. Specifically, `σ` is the distance where the repulsive and attractive *terms* of the potential cancel each other out. You control this with the 'Sigma (σ)' slider. Its units are distance, typically nanometers or Angstroms.

Dissecting the Two Terms

The elegance of the Lennard-Jones formula lies in its two-part structure, which mirrors the two physical forces at play.

The Repulsive Term: (σ/r)12

This part of the equation models the strong, short-range repulsion.

  • (σ/r): This ratio compares the atom's size `σ` to the separation `r`. When `r` is very small (much smaller than `σ`), this ratio becomes very large.
  • (...)^12: Raising this large ratio to the 12th power makes it astronomically large. This means the potential energy skyrockets to a large positive value, representing a massive repulsive force. This is the "wall" you see on the left side of the graph. The choice of the power 12 is largely for computational convenience. The attractive term is raised to the 6th power, and squaring a number (`(r^6)^2 = r^12`) is computationally faster than calculating another exponential, a significant saving in simulations with millions of particles. While not perfectly describing the underlying quantum mechanics, it creates a "stiff" enough repulsion to be a very good approximation for many simple atoms. This term represents the Pauli repulsion, preventing the collapse of matter.

The Attractive Term: -(σ/r)6

This part of the equation models the weaker, long-range attraction.

  • (σ/r): Again, we have this ratio. When `r` is large (larger than `σ`), this ratio is a small number less than 1.
  • (...)^6: Raising a small number to the 6th power makes it even smaller. This means the attractive effect diminishes rapidly with distance, but much more slowly than the repulsive term. The `r^6` dependence can be theoretically derived for the interaction between two fluctuating, induced dipoles (London dispersion forces), giving this part of the potential a strong physical basis.
  • The negative sign in front of this term is crucial. It makes the potential energy contribution negative, signifying attraction. It's what creates the "well" or "valley" in the potential curve.

The Equilibrium Distance (rmin)

The simulation shows a "valley" in the potential energy curve. The very bottom of this valley is the most important point. This is the position where the net force on the particles is zero. It's the most stable separation distance, often called the equilibrium distance or bond length, denoted as `r_min`. At this point, the repulsive push perfectly balances the attractive pull.

We can find this point using calculus by taking the derivative of the potential `V(r)` with respect to `r` and setting it to zero (since the slope is zero at a minimum). Doing so yields a fixed relationship:

rmin = 21/6 σ ≈ 1.122 σ

This is a remarkable result. It shows that for *any* pair of particles described by a Lennard-Jones potential, the most stable separation distance is always about 12.2% larger than the `σ` parameter. At this distance, the potential energy is exactly `-ε`. You can verify this in the simulation: set `r` to `1.122 * σ` and observe that the potential energy becomes equal to `-ε`.

Worked Examples

Let's solidify our understanding by plugging in some numbers. For simplicity in these examples, let's use a "normalized" system where ε = 1.0 energy unit and σ = 1.0 distance unit. The simulation plots V/ε vs r/σ, so these examples directly correspond to the default graph.

Example 1: Calculating Potential at Specific Distances

Question: Using ε = 1.0 and σ = 1.0, what is the potential energy V(r) at a) r = σ, b) r = rmin, and c) r = 2σ?

Formula: V(r) = 4 * 1.0 * [ (1.0/r)12 - (1.0/r)6 ]

a) At r = σ = 1.0:

V(1.0) = 4 * [ (1.0/1.0)12 - (1.0/1.0)6 ]
V(1.0) = 4 * [ 112 - 16 ]
V(1.0) = 4 * [ 1 - 1 ] = 0
Answer: At a distance `r = σ`, the potential energy is exactly zero. This is by definition of the `σ` parameter. You can check this on the simulator.

b) At r = rmin ≈ 1.122:

We know that rmin = 21/6σ. So (σ/rmin) = 2-1/6.

(σ/rmin)6 = (2-1/6)6 = 2-1 = 0.5
(σ/rmin)12 = ((σ/rmin)6)2 = (0.5)2 = 0.25
Now, plugging these into the formula: V(rmin) = 4 * [ 0.25 - 0.5 ]
V(rmin) = 4 * [ -0.25 ] = -1.0
Answer: At the equilibrium distance, the potential energy is exactly -ε. This is the minimum possible energy for the system, representing the most stable state.

c) At r = 2σ = 2.0:

V(2.0) = 4 * [ (1.0/2.0)12 - (1.0/2.0)6 ]
V(2.0) = 4 * [ (0.5)12 - (0.5)6 ]
(0.5)6 = 1/64 = 0.015625
(0.5)12 = 1/4096 ≈ 0.000244
V(2.0) = 4 * [ 0.000244 - 0.015625 ]
V(2.0) = 4 * [ -0.015381 ] ≈ -0.0615
Answer: At twice the `σ` distance, the potential is negative but very close to zero. This shows how quickly the interaction strength fades with distance.

Example 2: Calculating the Force from the Potential

The force between the particles is the negative gradient (the negative slope) of the potential energy curve. The formula is F(r) = -dV/dr.

Question: Find the formula for the force F(r) and calculate it at r = rmin.

Derivation:

V(r) = 4ε [ σ12r-12 - σ6r-6 ]
To find the derivative dV/dr, we use the power rule (d/dx xn = nxn-1):
dV/dr = 4ε [ σ12(-12r-13) - σ6(-6r-7) ]
dV/dr = 4ε [ -12σ12/r13 + 6σ6/r7 ]
The force is F(r) = -dV/dr:
F(r) = -4ε [ -12σ12/r13 + 6σ6/r7 ]
F(r) = 4ε [ 12σ12/r13 - 6σ6/r7 ]
We can factor out some terms to make it cleaner: F(r) = (24ε/r) [ 2(σ/r)12 - (σ/r)6 ]

Calculation at r = rmin ≈ 1.122σ:

At this point, the force should be zero. Let's check. We already know that at rmin, (σ/r)6 = 0.5 and (σ/r)12 = 0.25. F(rmin) = (24ε/rmin) [ 2(0.25) - (0.5) ]
F(rmin) = (24ε/rmin) [ 0.5 - 0.5 ] = 0
Answer: As expected, the force at the bottom of the potential well is exactly zero. A positive force value indicates repulsion, and a negative force value indicates attraction.

Many Analogies for the Lennard-Jones Potential

Abstract mathematical formulas can be difficult to grasp. Analogies help connect these concepts to everyday experiences.

1. The Skateboarder in a Half-Pipe

This is perhaps the most powerful analogy. Imagine the potential energy curve is a physical ramp or a valley.

  • The particle's position `r` is the skateboarder's location along the ground.
  • The potential energy `V` is the height of the ramp at that location.
  • The Bottom of the Valley (r = rmin): This is the lowest point. A skateboarder will naturally come to rest here. It's the most stable, lowest-energy state. No forces are acting on the skateboarder (ignoring friction).
  • The Steep Inner Wall (r < σ): This is the very steep part of the ramp on the left. Trying to push the skateboarder up this wall is extremely difficult; it requires a lot of energy, and the skateboarder will be pushed back forcefully. This represents the strong short-range repulsion.
  • The Gentle Outer Slope (r > σ): This is the long, gentle slope on the right. If the skateboarder is on this slope, it will gently roll back towards the bottom of the valley. This represents the long-range attraction, which gets weaker and weaker the further away you go.

2. Two Magnets with a Spring

Imagine two magnets that are oriented to attract each other. They want to snap together. Now, imagine there's a very stiff spring between them.

  • The Magnets' Attraction: This represents the `-(σ/r)⁶` attractive term, always trying to pull the two objects together.
  • The Stiff Spring: This represents the `(σ/r)¹²` repulsive term. You can pull the magnets apart, stretching the spring (this is like the attractive force). But you can only push them together until the spring is fully compressed, at which point it exerts a massive outward force, preventing them from getting any closer.
  • Equilibrium: The final resting distance between the magnets is the point where the magnetic pull is perfectly balanced by the spring's push. This is `r_min`.

3. The "Personal Space" Social Analogy

Think of atoms as people at a party.

  • Attraction: People don't want to be completely alone in a corner. There's a general social "pull" to be near other people to chat. This is the van der Waals attraction. It's nice to be a few feet away from someone to have a conversation.
  • Repulsion: However, no one wants a stranger standing right up in their face, invading their personal space. If someone gets too close, you instinctively back away. This is the powerful Pauli repulsion.
  • Equilibrium Distance (`r_min`): This is the comfortable conversational distance. You are close enough to interact socially (attraction) but far enough away not to be uncomfortable (repulsion).
  • Sigma (`σ`): This might be the distance at which you first start to feel slightly awkward, where the pleasure of the social interaction is cancelled out by the discomfort of someone being a bit too close. The net "energy" is zero, but the force is still trying to pull you into a more comfortable (lower energy) distance.

Intuition & Visual Metaphors

The Tug-of-War Between Two Superpowers

Visualize the interaction as a tug-of-war between two competing superpowers: a giant, short-tempered strongman and a patient, long-armed weakling.

  • The Repulsive Strongman (`r^12` term) has incredibly powerful but very short arms. He can only exert his force when the particles are almost touching. When they are, he pushes them apart with immense power.
  • The Attractive Weakling (`r^6` term) has very long arms. His pull is much weaker, but its reach is far greater. He is always gently pulling the particles together.
The Battle:
  • Far Apart (Large r): The strongman can't reach. The weakling is the only one in the game, gently pulling the particles closer. The net force is attractive.
  • Getting Closer: As the particles get closer, the weakling's pull gets a bit stronger. The potential energy drops.
  • The Sweet Spot (r = rmin): The particles are now close enough that the strongman can just barely touch them. His outward push perfectly balances the weakling's inward pull. This is the point of zero force, the equilibrium.
  • Too Close (r < rmin): The strongman gets a solid grip. His terrifying power completely overwhelms the weakling's gentle pull. The net force is now strongly repulsive, pushing the particles apart.

Understanding the Graph's Shape

Let's look at the graph not as a math plot, but as a story.

  • The Far Right (r → ∞): The line is flat at zero. The particles are too far apart to feel each other. They are strangers. The potential energy is zero.
  • The Gentle Dip: As you bring them closer from the right, the potential energy dips below zero. This dip *is* the attraction. A system in a negative energy state is "bound"—it takes energy to break it apart. The deeper the dip (controlled by ε), the stronger the bond.
  • The Minimum Point: This is the bottom of the story, the most stable state. The particles have found their ideal separation. They are "happy" here.
  • The Steep Climb: Moving left from the minimum, the energy rises sharply and crosses zero at `r = σ`. The particles are now in a repulsive state. You are putting energy *into* the system by forcing them together, just like compressing a spring stores energy. The system is unstable and will release this energy by pushing the particles apart as soon as it can.
  • The Wall: The curve goes almost vertical. The universe is telling you, "You shall not pass!" The energy cost to push the particles any closer is enormous.

Advanced Extensions and Applications

The Lennard-Jones potential is not just a theoretical toy; it is a workhorse of computational science.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulation

The most significant use of the LJ potential is in Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations. Imagine a box filled with millions of particles (e.g., Argon atoms).

  1. For a single particle, we can calculate the small force exerted on it by every other particle in the box by summing up all the pairwise Lennard-Jones forces.
  2. Once we have the total force F on the particle, we know its acceleration a from Newton's second law (F=ma).
  3. Knowing the acceleration, we can predict the particle's new velocity and position a tiny fraction of a second (a "timestep," often femtoseconds) into the future.
  4. We repeat this process for every particle in the box for millions of timesteps.
The result is a movie of how atoms move and interact. By analyzing the particles' positions and velocities, we can calculate macroscopic properties like pressure, temperature, and density. We can watch crystals melt, liquids boil, and glasses form, all based on this simple underlying potential. It's a powerful bridge from the micro to the macro world.

Modeling Different States of Matter

  • Gases: At high temperatures and low densities, the particles are far apart and moving fast. Their kinetic energy is much larger than the potential well depth ε. They mostly fly past each other, only feeling weak attractive forces during brief encounters. The LJ model can predict the behavior of real gases.
  • Liquids: At lower temperatures and higher densities, the particles are constantly interacting, rolling around in each other's potential wells. They don't have enough energy to escape the collective attraction, but they have too much to settle into fixed positions. The LJ liquid is a canonical model system in statistical mechanics.
  • Solids: At very low temperatures, the particles lack the kinetic energy to escape their potential wells. They arrange themselves into a regular, repeating crystal lattice (typically a face-centered cubic structure for LJ particles) and vibrate around their equilibrium positions (`r_min`).

Beyond Lennard-Jones: More Realistic Potentials

The LJ potential is a great approximation, but it's not perfect. For more complex systems, scientists use more sophisticated potentials:

  • Buckingham Potential: Replaces the harsh `r^12` repulsive term with a more physically accurate exponential function, `exp(-r)`. This is computationally more expensive.
  • Morse Potential: Often used to model true covalent chemical bonds, as it includes a feature that allows bonds to break.
  • Force Fields (CHARMM, AMBER, GROMOS): For simulating proteins, DNA, and other complex biological molecules, scientists use "force fields." These are huge collections of different potential energy functions. They include Lennard-Jones terms for van der Waals interactions, but also terms for bond stretching, angle bending, and electrostatic interactions between charged atoms. They are parameterized to reproduce experimental data with high fidelity.

Q&A

1. Why use the powers 12 and 6? Are they special?

The `r^6` term for attraction has a solid theoretical justification. It describes the dipole-dipole interaction between atoms due to quantum fluctuations in their electron clouds (London dispersion forces). The `r^12` term for repulsion, however, is a matter of mathematical convenience. The true repulsive force is a very complex quantum mechanical effect (related to the Pauli exclusion principle) that is better modeled by an exponential function. However, `r^12` is very steep, providing a good "wall," and critically, it is simply the square of the `r^6` term. In early computer simulations, calculating `(x^6)^2` was much faster than calculating `exp(x)`. This computational shortcut allowed for much larger and longer simulations and became a standard. While not perfectly physical, it's an excellent and efficient approximation for many systems, especially noble gases like Argon, for which it was originally developed.

2. What are typical real-world values for ε and σ?

These parameters vary depending on the atom or molecule. They are typically determined by fitting simulation results to experimental data, like the boiling point or viscosity of a substance.

  • For Argon (Ar), a classic example, `σ` is about 3.405 Angstroms (3.405 x 10-10 meters), and `ε` is about 1.654 x 10-21 Joules.
  • For Nitrogen (N2), `σ` is about 3.698 Angstroms, and `ε` is about 1.31 x 10-21 Joules.
The value of `ε` is often expressed in terms of temperature by dividing by the Boltzmann constant (kB). For Argon, ε/kB ≈ 119.8 K. This means the thermal energy of an atom at ~120 Kelvin is comparable to the depth of the potential well, giving a rough estimate of its boiling point (Argon's is 87 K).

3. Is the Lennard-Jones potential always accurate?

No, it is an approximation and has significant limitations. It works best for neutral, spherical, non-polar particles, like noble gases (Argon, Krypton). It fails to accurately describe systems with:

  • Metals: Metallic bonding involves a "sea" of delocalized electrons, which is a collective effect not captured by pairwise potentials.
  • Ionic Systems: Interactions in salts (like NaCl) are dominated by the strong electrostatic Coulomb force between charged ions (Na+ and Cl-), which has a much longer range (`1/r`) dependence.
  • Polar Molecules & Hydrogen Bonds: Molecules like water (H2O) have permanent dipoles and form strong, directional hydrogen bonds. The simple LJ potential cannot account for these orientation-dependent interactions. More complex force fields are needed.
  • Covalent Bonds: The LJ potential models the weak forces *between* molecules, not the strong covalent bonds *within* a molecule that hold atoms together.

4. Why is the minimum potential energy negative?

This is a convention in physics, but a very meaningful one. Potential energy is always defined relative to a zero point. For interatomic potentials, we set the zero point at an infinite separation distance (r → ∞). At this distance, the atoms cannot feel each other, so their interaction energy is zero. When the atoms come closer and fall into the potential well, they form a stable, bound state. The system releases energy (often as heat) to enter this stable state. Therefore, its energy must be *lower* than the zero reference energy, hence it is negative. To break the bond and separate the atoms back to infinity, you have to put that energy back *into* the system. So, a negative potential energy signifies stability and binding.

5. What is the difference between the potential energy (V) and the force (F)?

Energy and force are two different but deeply related concepts. The force is the agent of change, while the potential energy describes the state of the system. The mathematical relationship is F = -dV/dr, which means the force is the negative of the slope of the potential energy graph.

  • Imagine you are on a hilly landscape (the potential graph). Your potential energy is your altitude. The force you feel is the pull of gravity, which depends on the steepness (slope) of the hill.
  • Where the slope is negative (downhill), the force is positive (pushing you in the positive `r` direction). In the LJ graph, this corresponds to the repulsive region.
  • Where the slope is positive (uphill), the force is negative (pulling you in the negative `r` direction). This is the attractive region.
  • Where the slope is zero (bottom of the valley), the force is zero. This is the equilibrium point.

6. Can I use this potential to model a chemical bond?

You generally should not. The Lennard-Jones potential is designed to model non-covalent interactions, specifically van der Waals forces. These are the weak forces that hold molecules together in a liquid or solid. A true chemical bond (like the O-H bond in water or the C-C bond in diamond) is a covalent bond, which is much stronger and involves the sharing of electrons. Covalent bonds are better described by other models, like the Morse potential, which more accurately captures the energy profile and allows for bond dissociation. An LJ well depth (`ε`) is typically 10-100 times *weaker* than the energy of a covalent bond.

7. Why does the graph plot V/ε versus r/σ?

This is a clever scientific trick called nondimensionalization. By dividing the potential `V` by `ε` and the distance `r` by `σ`, we remove the specific units and parameters of any single substance. The resulting equation, V* = 4 [ (1/r*)12 - (1/r*)6 ] where V* = V/ε and r* = r/σ, is universal. The graph you see in the simulation is this single, universal curve. This means this one graph is valid for Argon, Nitrogen, Methane, etc. To apply it to a specific substance, you simply "re-dimensionalize" it by stretching the y-axis by that substance's `ε` and the x-axis by its `σ`. This principle of corresponding states is a powerful concept in thermodynamics and physics.

8. What is the Pauli Exclusion Principle and how does it cause repulsion?

The Pauli Exclusion Principle is a fundamental rule of quantum mechanics. In simple terms, it states that two identical fermions (a class of particles that includes electrons) cannot occupy the same quantum state simultaneously. An electron's state is defined by properties like its energy level and its spin. When two atoms are brought very close together, their electron clouds begin to overlap. If this happens, you would have electrons from both atoms trying to occupy the same region of space with the same energy levels. The principle forbids this. To avoid this forbidden state, the electrons are forced into higher energy orbitals, which requires a massive amount of energy. This required energy manifests as a powerful repulsive force pushing the nuclei apart. The `(σ/r)^12` term is a crude but effective mathematical caricature of this profound quantum effect.

9. How do you get macroscopic properties like the boiling point from this potential?

This is the magic of statistical mechanics and simulation. You can't determine a boiling point from just two atoms. But if you use a computer to simulate millions of atoms interacting via the Lennard-Jones potential (a technique called Molecular Dynamics, as described in the Advanced Extensions section), you can measure macroscopic properties of the whole system. To find the boiling point, you would run a series of simulations at different temperatures. You would start with a liquid state and gradually increase the temperature. At low temperatures, the particles stick together. As you increase the temperature, they vibrate more and more. The boiling point is the temperature at which the particles gain enough kinetic energy to overcome the attractive potential (`ε`) of their neighbors and escape into a gaseous phase. By observing this phase transition in the simulation, you can determine the substance's boiling point as predicted by the model.

10. What happens if I make ε very large?

Making ε very large means you are increasing the "stickiness" or binding energy between the particles. You can see this in the simulation as the potential well getting much deeper. In the real world, this would correspond to a substance with very strong intermolecular forces. Such a substance would have a very high boiling point and a high heat of vaporization, because you need to supply a lot of thermal energy to separate the molecules from each other. For example, a substance with a large `ε` is more likely to be a liquid or even a solid at room temperature, while a substance with a small `ε` is more likely to be a gas.

11. What is the physical meaning of the area under the force curve?

This is an excellent question that connects force, energy, and work. The force is F(r) = -dV/dr. If we rearrange this, we get dV = -F(r)dr. Integrating both sides tells us that the change in potential energy, ΔV, is the negative of the integral of force over distance. The integral of a function is the area under its curve. Therefore, the area under the force-distance curve represents the work done by the interaction force. Specifically, the work done by the force when moving a particle from a distance r1 to r2 is W = ∫F(r)dr. This work is equal to the negative of the change in potential energy, W = -ΔV = -(V(r2) - V(r1)). For example, the work required for an external agent to pull two particles apart from their equilibrium distance (rmin) to infinity (r=∞) is equal to the well depth, ε. This is because you must do work against the attractive force of the potential well. This work is stored as potential energy, increasing it from -ε to 0.
